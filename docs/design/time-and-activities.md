# Time, energy and activities

**Summary.** Time is your main budget. Where you spend it decides who you see
every day, what you get good at, and which cards end up in your deck. A routine
plays most of the day for you; the game stops only when a decision matters.

## Calendar

- **Prototype:** one fall semester, about 16 school weeks. **Full game:**
  freshman year, possibly later years.
- **Each school day:**
  1. Before school — hallway, courtyard, library.
  2. Morning classes — three periods.
  3. Lunch — pick a table or a place.
  4. Afternoon classes — three periods.
  5. After school — activity practice, hang out somewhere, or go home.
  6. Evening — homework, practice a skill, follow an interest, rest, or
     accept an invite.
- **Weekends:** one or two plans (a hangout, a party, a game, an event) plus
  rest or practice.

## Classes

- **The schedule is assigned at the start,** with a couple of elective choices.
  Classes are where you see the same people again and again, and the people
  sitting next to you are who you end up talking to — a big source of
  friendships, as in real life.
- **You choose where you sit in each class.** Classrooms have 36 desks for
  about 25 students, so there are open desks around the room. You pick each
  class's seat the first time you walk into it, so by 3rd period you may
  already know people from 1st and 2nd — the room shows their name tags, and
  the panel tells you who's there. Seats stay put, and you can move any
  morning ("Change class seats"). Hovering a desk shows who you'd be next
  to. In week 8 the afternoon teachers assign a new seating chart; you can move
  again after.
- **You choose who to walk to class with.** After hanging out before school,
  you pick someone from your 1st-period class to walk with — people you know
  first, plus a couple of strangers — or walk alone. Someone who likes you says
  yes; with anyone else it's a gamble (your confidence, standing, and whether
  your shirt is their thing). A no isn't a scene: they're just walking with
  someone else. A yes is a quick two-turn card conversation that a few
  classmates see.
- **Talking in class is limited and risky** (the teacher is watching). Group
  projects and lab partners come up as storyteller events.

## Schoolwork versus popularity

The fastest ways to climb — hanging out after school and in the evening,
weekend plans — all cost study time. Schoolwork pays
back in slower, steadier ways.

- **Grades** follow effort: each school night you either study or don't, and
  grades drift toward what your effort and academic ability allow.
- **Studying means not seeing friends that night.** You hear stories later,
  or from someone else.
- **Parents ground you if grades fall below their bar.** Stricter parents set
  a higher bar and are quicker to act. Grounded means no after-school
  hanging out, no weekend plans or parties, and no evening phone or friends for a week —
  a big social cost, so keeping grades up protects your social time.
- **Knowing the material earns standing through helping.** Students who are
  struggling ask whoever sitting near them knows the work best — more often
  in the week before and the week of midterms and finals — so people come to
  you; you don't have to offer. Classmates also ask for help in ordinary class
  chats. Help builds trust and gratitude, and earns respect, most from people
  who admire being smart.
- **Help creates a favor owed.** The person you helped shows you more
  deference in public, is glad to sit with you, and is more likely to back you
  up if you're picked on. The debt fades over time, and backing you up pays it
  down.
- **Grades are a constraint and a source of standing, not a score to chase.**

## Activities

Prototype set of four: **a fall sports team (soccer), band (which also plays
at games), drama (the fall play), robotics club.** Academics has no activity
on purpose: being good at school earns standing by helping people, not by
public competition (see [schoolwork](#schoolwork-versus-popularity)). Humor
and style show in everyday conversation.

- **Getting in.** Tryouts or auditions with a skill threshold and limited spots
  (soccer, drama), or open entry (band if you play an instrument, robotics).
- **Showing up.** Practices are on set days. Missing one costs trust with the
  coach and teammates; showing up is a costly signal that builds loyalty.
- **Skill** grows with practice and with evening practice.
- **Standing inside the activity** — starter, lead role, captain — earns
  respect inside and outside it.
- **Culture.** Each activity develops its own norms from its members and
  overlaps with friend groups.
- **Big moments.** Games, performances and competitions are witnessed by many
  people and can swing standing a lot.

## What each choice gives and costs

Every way to spend time has several benefits and several costs. All of these
are in the headless simulation except where marked *(design only)*.

| Choice | Gives | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Soccer** | Respect from people who admire athletes, especially at games; fitness, which means more energy, faster recovery from stress, and steadier composure; a tight team | Tryouts (you may not make it); four practices a week plus games |
| **Band** | Respect for arts; plays at games, so it's seen often; a big group | Instrument practice at home some evenings, so less homework and time with friends |
| **Drama** | Respect for arts; poise, which keeps you steady when challenged in public; the fall play is a big public moment | Auditions; rehearsal crunch before the play takes evenings and adds stress |
| **Robotics** | Respect for tech; tech help is a way to be useful | One public moment a semester; build-season crunch |
| **Studying (evening)** | Grades, which keep you from being grounded and make you the person classmates ask for help, especially near exams | Not seeing friends that night |
| **Hanging out in the evening** | Trust and closeness with a couple of close friends; you hear their stories in person | Only possible if you have close friends (people you like and trust, who like you back) who are free that night; no homework or practice that night |
| **Practicing a skill (evening)** | Your activity's skill or your strongest ability improves; you learn more about an interest, which gives you more to talk about | Nothing social happens |
| **Phone (evening)** | Tonight only: a little stress relief, catching up on stories, light contact with friends | Builds a habit that pulls you back; costs sleep (less energy tomorrow); adds background stress; makes homework less likely and less effective; online contact builds no trust; a habit means checking your phone while with friends, so you say less and they feel less close to you. Over a semester it always leaves you worse off |
| **Hanging out after school** | Trust and closeness with the friends you spend it with | Time not spent on an activity or homework |
| **Behind the gym** (the rule-breaking crowd) | The easiest group to get into — they take almost anyone, and sneaking off together bonds fast; a refuge for kids with few friends | Skipping last period costs grades; passersby see you and most respect you less; you get tagged a troublemaker; sometimes a teacher catches you (detention, grades, maybe grounded); your friends' habits rub off on you |
| **Rest (evening)** | Less stress and more energy for tomorrow | Nothing social happens |

## Mixing beats repeating

No single way of spending time should win on its own; the best plan is a mix
that responds to how things are going.

- **Repetition wears out.** Each evening of the same thing in the last six
  pays off less. Homework wears out slowly (doing it most nights is normal and
  keeps working); the same friends every night, or the same practice every
  night, wear out fast. For time with friends, everyone in the group's recent
  evenings count.
- **Things feed each other.** Being rested makes you more talkative and
  better company; late nights out cost energy the next day (more for
  introverts); a hard practice burns off stress.
- **Neglect catches up.** School nights without homework while your grades are
  near your parents' bar add stress. Grades that fall too far mean sitting out
  your activity until they recover ("no pass, no play").

## Energy, stress and knowledge

- **Social energy.** Every interaction costs some (less if you're fit). It
  recovers overnight (more if you're fit), and, for extroverts, with friends. The player's own personality sets the rates.
  Running low adds weak "tired" cards to your hand.
- **Stress** comes from conflicts, bad grades, humiliation and overscheduling.
  High stress lowers composure.
- **Knowledge.** Interest knowledge (a sport, a game, a show, a music scene)
  grows with evening time spent on it and with time around people into it. It
  unlocks cards about that topic — the "sacrificed time to know the group's
  stuff" signal.

## A school day, as you play it

Every social decision is yours; classwork happens in the background.

1. **Get dressed** — a look and a kind of shirt (plain, funny graphic tee, fan shirt, or hoodie).
2. **Before school** — you start inside the front doors and nobody comes to
   get you. People stand wherever they like: some on their own (often the
   reserved or anxious ones, and anyone who doesn't know people yet), some in
   pairs, some in groups of three to eight. Click anyone on the map, or pick
   from a short list, to walk up to them. Someone on their own is usually glad
   of the company; a group of strangers might not make room. Then a short
   chat. You can also change your class seats here.

   How the crowd forms: standing alone is one real option for everyone
   (not one option per empty spot), big circles are harder to be part of so
   people drift into smaller groups, and walking up to strangers is something
   few people do. Measured in one school: on the first day about 5 people stand
   alone, 6–11 pairs, and groups of 3–6; by mid-semester, friend groups of 6–8,
   a few pairs, and a few people still on their own.
3. **Walk to 1st period** — with a classmate, or alone.
4. **Morning classes** — one at a time. The first time you walk into each,
   you pick your seat. One class a morning has a moment: whisper to someone
   next to you, or keep your head down (the teacher might tell you to quiet
   down).
5. **Lunch** — pick a table, then the main card conversation.
6. **Afternoon classes** — another whisper-or-focus moment.
7. **After school** — your activity, hanging out, behind the gym, or home.
   Wherever you go, the talking is yours to play:
   - **At practice** your teammates talk in little groups between drills. Pick
     a group (from the list, or click someone on the map) and play a
     three-turn conversation, with the rest of the team in earshot — or just
     focus on practice.
   - **Behind the gym** the same, with the rule-breaking crowd. Whoever you
     talk with there bonds with you fast (a shared risk), and anyone walking
     past sees you there.
   - **Hanging out** with the friends who said yes is a longer, private
     conversation: four turns, and nobody else hears it.

   The simulation leaves your conversation for the game to play rather than
   playing it for you, so it only happens once.
8. **Evening** — homework, friends (you pick who to call; they come if they're
   free), practice, rest, or the phone.
9. **Weekends** — friends sometimes invite you over on Saturday (decided on
   Friday); saying no costs a little.

Events from the storyteller appear as decision cards at the start of a day.

## Playing fast (planned)

- **Routine.** Defaults for the routine choices (usual table, usual seat,
  usual evening) so quiet days go quickly.
- **Speed controls** like a city sim: pause, play, fast.
- **Conversations** are where you spend real thinking time.
