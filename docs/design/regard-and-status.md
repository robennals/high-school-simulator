# Regard, status, and the status–liking tension

**Summary.** What each student thinks of each other student is a handful of
private judgments plus a public stance. Status is the public deference you get.
Status can rest on real regard (prestige) or on fear (dominance). The gap
between public stance and private opinion is what makes fear-based status
fragile, and that tension is a central mechanic.

## What A privately thinks of B

- **Affection** (−1 to 1) — do I like being around them?
- **Trust** (−1 to 1) — would they back me up and keep my secrets? Loyal to me?
- **Respect** (−1 to 1) — do they have abilities or access that I value or that
  could help me? Covers both admiration and usefulness.
- **Fear** (0 to 1) — can they hurt me?
- **Familiarity** (0 to 1) — how well do I know them? Low familiarity means new
  information moves the other judgments a lot and the judgments are uncertain.

A also holds a **belief about B's standing**: how A thinks everyone else treats
B. This is A's estimate of B's power to help or hurt through influence.

## What A shows publicly

A's **public stance** toward B mixes A's private regard with what A believes
the people A watches think of B (see
[who follows whom](signals-and-gossip.md#who-follows-whom)), plus a boost for
people A fears:

```
public = (1 − conformity) × private + conformity × perceivedConsensus + fearBonus
```

This produces the "laugh at the bully's jokes while privately disliking him"
behavior (economists call it preference falsification).

## Status

- **Status is public deference.** It shows in who seeks your company, laughs at
  your jokes, gives way to you, and invites you. It is computed like PageRank:
  deference from high-status people counts for more.
- **Status is local.** Each student has their own belief about everyone's
  standing, built from what they see and hear. A theater star may be invisible
  to the football team.
- **Two levels:** standing within your group, and standing across your grade or
  the school.

## The foundation: prestige versus dominance

For each student, the deference they get splits three ways:

- **Liked** — given by people who enjoy them and trust them.
- **Admired** (prestige) — given by people who respect what they're good at.
  You can admire someone without liking them.
- **Feared** (dominance) — given by people with no private regard for them:
  out of fear, or just going along with the crowd.

The player's status is drawn as a bar sitting on a three-part **foundation**.
"Admired but not liked" (the gifted, aloof kid) and "liked but not admired"
(everyone's nice friend with no clout) are different positions with different
risks. Only the feared part is fragile.

## The tension mechanic

The fastest ways to gain status mostly spend liking:

- **Association.** Being seen with higher-status people raises your status, and
  being seen with lower-status friends lowers it. Ditching an old friend's lunch
  table for the "better" one works — and the friend saw it, and so did everyone
  who values loyalty.
- **Exclusion.** A group's status rises when it is hard to get into. Every
  person kept out likes the group a little less.
- **Put-downs.** A put-down in front of an audience moves status from the target
  to you, if the audience laughs. It costs the affection of the target and the
  target's friends, and it adds fear.
- **Showing off.** Wins status from people who don't know better and costs
  respect from people who do.

The ways to build liking mostly cost status or time:

- spending time with lower-status people,
- defending an unpopular kid,
- admitting a mistake,
- including someone who was left out.

The skilled play is building status *on* liking: the prestige route, plus
choosing the moments when spending some liking on status is worth it.

## Why fear-based status collapses (the cascade)

- Fear-backed deference is a public stance sitting on top of a hostile private
  opinion.
- Each student decides who is in or out partly by watching a handful of others
  (see [who follows whom](signals-and-gossip.md#who-follows-whom)). Once enough
  of the people they watch drop their deference, they drop it too.
- **Triggers:** the dominant student is publicly humiliated, loses a key ally,
  is caught lying, gets in trouble, or is stood up to by someone widely
  respected.
- Once triggered, public stances snap toward private opinion across many
  students at once. Status that took months can fall in days.
- Prestige-backed status doesn't collapse this way, because there is no hidden
  gap to snap back.

This is how the rules enforce "manipulation wins short term, loses long term."
The player can also see a wobbling bully coming, through gossip that reveals
private dislike, and decide whether to stand up, wait, or join the pile-on.

## Reputation tags

On top of the numbers, students carry shared labels built from repeated stories:
*funny, fake, snitch, loyal, try-hard, mean, nice, weird, jock, brain*. Tags
spread through gossip and tint how ambiguous acts get read (a "mean" kid's
tease reads as an attack). They are hard to remove: it takes several strong
signals the other way.

## Investment balance (trying too hard)

For every relationship, the simulation tracks each side's recent investment:
approaches, invites, favors, compliments. If A invests much more than B gives
back, B's respect for A slips, and onlookers may tag A *try-hard* or *needy*.
The effect is larger when the effort is visible and when A has lower status.
Being somewhat less available raises your value.

The many forms of trying too hard all run through this rule or the consistency
check in [signals](signals-and-gossip.md#how-a-witness-reads-an-event):

- laughing too hard or agreeing too quickly (repeated cheap support),
- name-dropping (bragging about who you know),
- a sudden style change around people who barely know you,
- showing off knowledge beyond what the group wants,
- texting or approaching too often,
- acting differently depending on who is watching.
