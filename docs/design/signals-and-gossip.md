# Signals, gossip and groups

**Summary.** Every action becomes an event that an audience sees. Each witness
interprets it through their own lens, and some retell it, with distortion and a
record of who told whom. Groups are never defined anywhere: they emerge from who
spends time with whom. Bullying is a status play whose payoff depends on the
audience and on whether the target has backing.

## Events

Every action is an event with:

- the **actor**, the **act**, and the **target** or targets,
- **witnesses** — everyone within sight or earshot, which depends on the place,
- **publicness** — alone, small group, or crowd,
- **explicitness** — from a hint to saying it outright,
- **cost to the actor** — time, energy, risk, bridges burned,
- **content tags** — topic, stance, and which groups the content pleases or
  offends.

## How a witness reads an event

For each witness:

1. **Interpret.** Many acts are ambiguous: a tease can be friendly or mean. The
   witness picks a reading at random, weighted by the act itself, their
   affection for the actor, the actor's reputation tags, their own neuroticism
   (assuming bad intent), and their relationship to the target.
2. **Update about the actor.** A warm act raises affection. A brave defense
   raises respect and trust among people who like the target. Putting down
   someone the witness likes lowers affection and raises fear. If the witness
   admires edginess, a put-down may read as funny and raise respect.
3. **Update about the target.** A target who visibly takes a put-down drops in
   the witness's estimate of their standing. A target who deflects well rises.
4. **Scale by cost.** How much the update moves depends on what the act cost
   the actor. A compliment costs nothing. Defending someone in front of a hostile
   crowd costs a lot. Costly signals include:
   - showing up repeatedly (time),
   - keeping a secret when sharing it would pay off,
   - turning down an invite from a rival group,
   - saying something that burns you with another group,
   - having built a skill the group needs,
   - showing up when it's inconvenient.
5. **Check consistency.** Students remember what each person said, to whom, and
   about whom. If a witness sees or hears someone saying opposite things to
   different audiences, that person gets flagged *two-faced*: a sharp trust
   drop, and the tag spreads.

## Belonging signals: all speech has social meaning

- **Opinions.** Voicing an opinion that matches the audience's norm signals
  belonging. It's a stronger signal if it would offend another group — and if a
  member of that group overhears, or hears about it, the cost is real.
- **Knowing the group's stuff.** Deep knowledge of the group's interest signals
  investment, because the knowledge took time to build. It's only possible if
  you have put in that time.
- **Help.** Real help raises respect and trust, and creates a favor owed.

## Clothes

What you wear is a signal everyone can read before you say a word.

- **Your look** — sporty, preppy, alt, artsy, techy, or plain. Dressing like a
  group reads as "one of us" to its members, and slightly as "not one of us"
  to conformists elsewhere. It makes joining their table a little easier.
- **A look only means something if it's distinctive.** The more of the school
  wears a look, the less it signals: dressing like a group counts for less, and
  dressing differently matters less, when everyone wears it. Once a look is
  everywhere, students who want to stand out move on from it — back to their
  own taste, or to something rarer — and conformists are slower to adopt a
  look that has stopped meaning anything. This keeps the school from settling
  on one style and makes fashions rise and fall.
- **A shirt about something** — the school team, a movie series, a pop star,
  a game, a band, a show. It states your opinion publicly, and it works as a
  conversation starter in both directions:
  - **Fans come to you.** People who love the thing are drawn to sit near you,
    are more likely to start chatting with you in class, and find it easy to
    walk up — your shirt hands them the opener ("wait, you like Static
    Lake?"). You don't have to take the risk of approaching.
  - **It's aimed.** Only fans are pulled in. People who mock the thing are put
    off and read you as out-group, so a shirt picks *which* people approach
    you, not just how many.
  - **When you approach,** fans at a table also make it easier for you to join.
  - **It has to go with your look.** A band tee doesn't go with a sporty
    outfit. Sporty: the school team. Preppy: Nova Ray (pop star) or Island
    Love (show). Alt: Static Lake (band) or Blockfront (game). Artsy: Static
    Lake or the Starfall movies. Techy: Blockfront or the Starfall movies.
    Low-key goes with anything. So if what you love doesn't match the crowd
    you dress like, you choose which one to show.
- **What kind of shirt.** Everyone wears a shirt; the kind decides how
  approachable you look. It is a trade-off between being easy to talk to and
  being left alone:

  | Shirt | Who comes to talk | Who teases it | Also |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | **Plain** | the usual number | nobody: nothing to grab onto | no help meeting people |
  | **Funny graphic tee** | more people who like funny, outgoing people (1.0–1.7×, about 1.2× on average) | edgy people | more interruptions in class (a small hit to grades), more tiring for reserved students |
  | **Fan shirt** | fans of the thing, many more; people who mock it, fewer | people who can't stand the thing (rarely in a friendly way) | the strongest pull, aimed at people who share your taste |
  | **Hoodie** | about 60% as many | fewer people tease you at all (half the time they pick someone else, or let it go) | fewer interruptions, better focus; slower to make friends |

  **A funny or fan tee is an opener.** Someone it draws often starts with
  your shirt ("Wait — you like Static Lake?", "Ha — where did you even find
  that shirt?"), which warms them to you more than an ordinary question. The
  same shirt is something to tease ("Nice shirt. Did you lose a bet?").

  No shirt is best for everyone: how much you want people to approach you
  depends on how outgoing you are, how much your grades are at risk, and
  how many friends you already have.
- **Choosing.** You pick your look and your shirt every morning, just like
  you choose the rest of your day; the game shows how common each look is.
  Simulated students settle on a kind of shirt each week: outgoing students
  lean toward graphic tees, reserved or stressed ones toward hoodies, and
  fans wear a shirt about what they love unless the people they watch mock
  it and they care what those people think. Conformists drift toward the look
  most of their close friends share, but everyone has their own taste and
  drifts back to it when their friends don't share one look.
- **Changing your look suddenly** around people who barely know you can read
  as trying too hard (see
  [regard and status](regard-and-status.md#investment-balance-trying-too-hard)).

## Gossip

- **Stories.** Notable events become stories: the event, how juicy it is, how
  distorted it has become, and the chain of who told whom. Juicy means it
  involves high-status people, conflict, broken norms, romance or humiliation.
- **Who tells.** In conversation, students may share stories. That's more
  likely when a story is juicier, when the teller has a gossip habit (low
  agreeableness, high extraversion, high status drive — being "in the know" is a
  status tool), and when teller and listener are close. People mostly avoid
  telling a story to the subject's close friends.
- **Who believes.** Belief = the listener's trust in the teller × how plausible
  the story is given what they already think of the subject, discounted if the
  teller is a known gossip or has a motive (a rival of the subject).
- **Effect.** Hearsay moves opinions less than seeing it firsthand (start at
  about 40% of a witness's update, scaled by belief).
- **Distortion.** Each retelling may exaggerate the story.
- **Provenance.** Listeners remember who told them. If the subject finds out,
  they learn the last link in the chain and can dig further back. Starting a
  hurtful story can be traced to you.
- **Speed target.** A juicy story should reach most of a grade within a few
  school days.

Gossip also spreads by phone: an evening on the phone means friends catch you up
on the latest stories. The phone is never a good long-term choice (see
[time and activities](time-and-activities.md#what-each-choice-gives-and-costs)). See
[decisions](decisions.md).

## Closeness needs time outside school

Time together at school — lunch, class, practice — can make people friendly,
but trust built that way tops out at a modest level. Becoming close takes
private time: after-school or evening hangouts, weekends, or costly signals
like standing up for someone or taking a risk together.

## Who follows whom

- Each student watches a small **reference set** of about 3–6 people: their
  closest friends and the highest-status members of their group.
- Their public stance toward anyone drifts toward the reference set's average
  public stance, weighted by their conformity.
- **Thresholds.** Each student has a personal threshold. Once that share of
  their reference set has turned on someone (or embraced them), they switch too.
  This produces cascades in both directions: someone suddenly being "in," or a
  sudden pile-on.
- **Leaders** are simply students whom many others watch. The player can only
  find them by observing.

## Groups

- **Never labeled in the data.** Each simulated week, the simulation finds
  clusters in the "hangout graph" (who chose to spend free time together, and
  how often) using a standard community-detection method. Students, the
  storyteller and the debrief use those clusters; the player never sees them
  directly.
- **Seeds:** feeder middle schools, activities, neighborhoods and bus routes,
  shared classes, shared interests and similar personalities (people befriend
  people like themselves).
- **Properties that emerge:**
  - *norms* — the members' average admired qualities and opinion stances,
  - *status* — how the rest of the grade defers to members,
  - *cohesion* (strength) — how dense the mutual trust is inside the group,
  - *leaders* — the members others in the group watch most,
  - *exclusivity* — how rarely they accept newcomers.
- **Bridges.** Students in two clusters are the most valuable contacts: one
  friendship there can open two groups.
- **Change.** Groups split when cohesion drops (say, two leaders fall out) and
  merge when activities keep throwing them together.

## Backing: why group strength protects you

When someone is targeted, each friend present may step in with a chance based on
their trust in the target, their loyalty, their courage (low neuroticism, their
own standing), and how little they fear the attacker. There is a bonus when
several members of a cohesive group are present, because groups move together.
Bullies estimate exactly this "protection" when choosing targets. So your safety
comes from the strength of your groups, not just how many friends you have.

## Bullying

- **Who bullies:** students with high status drive and low agreeableness, whose
  standing is in the middle and rising. They have something to gain. Research on
  status and aggression (Faris & Felmlee) finds aggression peaks below the very
  top of the hierarchy.
- **Targets:** someone with low protection, or a rival whose standing they want,
  in front of an audience they expect to laugh.
- **Payoff:** status from the audience's reaction, minus affection from those
  who sympathize. Bullies learn: if a target keeps deflecting or keeps being
  defended, they move on.
- **Forms:** put-downs, mocking, exclusion ("you can't sit here"), rumors, and
  rarely a mild shove. A shove makes nearly everyone who sees or hears about it
  like the shover less.
- **Defenses:** friends nearby; refusing the bully's frame (see
  [frame contests](conversation-cards.md#frame-contests)); not giving them a
  stage; telling an adult.

## Standing up for someone

- **It's a gamble.** Whether the bully backs off depends on your standing and
  composure versus theirs, how many people present are on your side, and luck.
- **If it works,** the bully loses face and some of the fear others have of
  them, and onlookers who like the target respect you.
- **If it backfires,** the bully turns on you: you're the one hurt and
  rattled, and occasionally it escalates to a shove.
- **Either way,** the person you stood up for trusts you a lot — even more if
  you took a hit for them — and the bully holds a grudge and is more likely to
  come after you later.

## Adults

- **Reporting works for serious things.** Repeated or physical bullying,
  reported to a teacher, gets the bully disciplined and keeps them off you for a
  while.
- **It has costs.** The bully's friends may resent you. How you do it matters:
  privately costs less than publicly.
- **Complaining about minor stuff costs you.** Reporting a one-off tease, or
  reporting often, earns a *snitch* tag and lost respect, and nothing happens to
  the other person.
- **Teachers are present** in classes, which suppresses put-downs there and
  stops shoves entirely.

## Romance

Gentle and PG, nothing physical — it feels like a special friendship.

- **Crushes** form from affection, respect and familiarity, and default to the
  other gender.
- **Asking climbs a ladder:** invite them along with a group, then a smaller
  group, then one-on-one, then ask them out. Each step is deniable except the
  last.
- **Skipping the ladder is risky.** Asking someone out publicly when you don't
  know how they feel pressures them to say no to protect their own image, and
  both of you lose face.
- **Couples** ("going out") are juicy stories, share standing by association,
  and sometimes break up, which reshuffles friend groups.
